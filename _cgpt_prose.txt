## Recommended framing

For CHI, describe the project as supporting student researchersâ ethical reconsiderationânot âmaking them feel guilty.â The strongest comparison is:

1. **âIf Nothing Changesâ** â a near-future ethical pre-mortem showing plausible negative consequences.
2. **âYou Could Be Anyoneâ** â a role-reversal and veil-of-ignorance walkthrough in which the researcher experiences the design as a participant, an indirectly affected person, and a junior research worker.

Use the same underlying AI study, harms, duration, and mitigation options in both conditions. Manipulate only temporal versus social perspective.

This recommendation follows a targeted, not systematic, search of ACM, IEEE, PubMed/PMC, and official ACM/SIGCHI sources.

## Literature rationale

Three findings particularly support the study:

- The central problem is often an **awareness-to-action gap**. HCI researchers recognize LLM-related privacy, consent, harmful-output, and intellectual-integrity concerns but frequently address them only partially, citing distributed responsibility and competing priorities ([Kapania et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3711000)). McKeeâs analysis similarly found low reporting rates for ethics review, consent, and compensation in AI research involving human data ([McKee, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/TTS.2024.3446183)).
- Providing rules alone may be insufficient. In one experiment with students and professional developers, consulting the ACM Code did not significantly change ethical decisions ([McNamara et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3236024.3264833)). An experiential intervention is therefore justified.
- Identifying concrete consequences is associated with better ethical forecasting and decisions ([Stenmark et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1525/jer.2011.6.1.25)). Value scenarios likewise recommend examining stakeholders, time, pervasiveness, systemic effects, and value implications ([Nathan et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1145/1240866.1241046)).
- For the second condition, perspective-taking can reduce bias through increased selfâother overlap ([Galinsky & Moskowitz, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.4.708)), but simple empathy can individualize structural problems. Davis instead proposes **role-taking**, which foregrounds interdependent roles, status, and power ([Davis, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3661790.3661800)). Veil-of-ignorance experiments involving AI governance also produced stronger concern for fairness and the worst-off ([Weidinger et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2213709120)).

## Common research-design vignette

Both conditions should begin with exactly the same proposal:

> **StressLens study.** A graduate student proposes a six-week study involving 80 university students. Participants submit weekly text or voice diaries about academic stress. A commercial LLM will infer stress levels, generate personalized coping suggestions, and code themes for publication.  
>   
> The consent form says that âautomated analysisâ will occur, but does not identify the LLM provider, possible provider retention, model uncertainty, or the fact that raw diaries may contain information about roommates, teachers, and family members. LLM feedback is sent directly to participants without human review. There is no crisis-escalation protocol. Participants can request deletion from the university database, but deletion from provider logs has not been verified.  
>   
> The student researcher believes the risk is minimal because names are not deliberately requested, participation lasts only six weeks, and the chatbot is intended to be supportive.

This case combines direct participants, nonparticipant stakeholders, research workers, a commercial AI supply chain, sensitive data, and unpredictable model behavior.

## The two conditions

| Dimension | Condition 1: If Nothing Changes | Condition 2: You Could Be Anyone |
|---|---|---|
| Main mechanism | Consequence forecasting | Role-taking, fairness, and power |
| Perspective | Third-person, near-future timeline | First-person, rotating stakeholder roles |
| Temporal horizon | One semesterâapproximately 12 weeks | Present-tense experience of the same events |
| Expected strength | Identifying causal chains and broader harms | Recognizing agency, vulnerability, and unfair burden |
| Likely outcome | More harms and indirect stakeholders identified | Greater willingness to accept costs to protect participants |
| Main risk | Fear, reactance, or implausible catastrophizing | Projecting oneâs assumptions onto affected communities |
| Mitigation | Plausible outcomes plus concrete corrective actions | Stakeholder-validated scripts; do not claim simulated experience equals lived experience |

### Condition 1: âIf Nothing Changesâ

Present an interactive, third-person timeline:

> **Week 2:** A participantâs diary names a roommate, describes the roommateâs medication, and recounts a conflict with a lecturer. The entire entry is transmitted to the LLM provider. Neither the roommate nor lecturer knows that their information is being processed.  
>   
> **Week 4:** A sarcastic statement is classified as a serious crisis. The participant receives an alarming, overconfident response. In another entry, a genuine expression of distress is treated as ordinary exam stress. No trained person reviews either output.  
>   
> **Week 6:** The provider updates its model. Similar diary entries now receive different risk labels, but the research team has not recorded the model version or preserved the earlier outputs.  
>   
> **Week 8:** A participant withdraws and asks for deletion. The university removes its copy but cannot confirm whether prompts remain in provider logs.  
>   
> **Week 12:** The junior research assistant discovers these incidents while preparing the dataset. The protocol contains no adverse-event procedure, and the assistant has neither clinical training nor authority to pause the study.

Then prompt:

> Assume the research aims remain unchanged. Which consequences were reasonably foreseeable, who carries each burden, and what must be changed before recruitment begins?

Importantly, provide actionable repairs immediately after the negative timeline. A threat-only condition may provoke defensiveness; the researcher should see that modification is feasible.

### Condition 2: âYou Could Be Anyoneâ

Use a first-person **role-reversal stakeholder walkthrough**. Before revealing the roles, tell the student:

> You must decide the rules of this study without knowing which position you will occupy. Any protection you omit may affect you.

The student then rotates through the same incidents:

> **You are the participant.** You select a fictional, prewritten diary entryânever your real personal information. The interface informs you only that automated analysis will occur. Your entry receives an incorrect risk label and an overconfident recommendation. When you request deletion, you learn that provider-log deletion is uncertain.  
>   
> **You are the roommate mentioned in the diary.** Your medication and relationship difficulties have been included in an AI prompt. You did not enroll, never saw a consent form, and have no obvious way to request deletion.  
>   
> **You are the junior research assistant.** You are expected to review thousands of outputs, including distressing content, but have no training, support, or authority to stop data collection.  
>   
> **You are the student researcher.** Your graduation and submission schedule benefit from launching immediately, while most risks and labor fall on people with less decision-making power.

Final prompt:

> Before learning which role will finally be yours, establish the protections you would require to accept every role. Now revise the protocol.

This is stronger than merely saying âimagine how the participant feels.â It redistributes exposure, authority, benefit, and burden. Calling it a **Role-Reversal Stakeholder Mirror** or **Moral Mirror** could provide a memorable CHI design contribution.

## Proposed comparative study

### Research questions

- **RQ1:** Which condition produces higher-quality ethical modifications to an AI-mediated human-participant research design?
- **RQ2:** Do the interventions work through different mechanismsâconsequence salience versus fairness, selfâother overlap, and power awareness?
- **RQ3:** Which intervention produces better near-transfer to a different AI research problem one week later?
- **RQ4:** When do the interventions cause defensiveness, superficial compliance, or misplaced confidence?

Avoid predicting that the innovative condition will necessarily be superior. Its overall advantage should remain an empirical question.

Reasonable directional hypotheses are:

- **H1:** The future-consequence condition will elicit a greater number and diversity of harms and causal pathways.
- **H2:** The role-reversal condition will produce more participant-protective choices when safeguards impose a delay, cost, or reduction in research scope.
- **H3:** Effects of Condition 1 will be associated with consequence salience and response efficacy; effects of Condition 2 will be associated with fairness, selfâother overlap, and recognition of power asymmetries.

### Participants and procedure

An illustrative target is approximately **N = 280**, or 140 per condition, pending a preregistered power analysis using pilot variance. Recruit masterâs students, doctoral students, and advanced undergraduates who have designedâor expect to designâresearch involving humans and AI.

Procedure:

1. Collect prior AI experience, ethics training, research stage, discipline, and jurisdiction.
2. Show the common proposal and obtain a baseline approval decision and initial protocol edits.
3. Randomly assign participants to one of the two conditions.
4. Let them revise the protocol through a redline interface.
5. Ask them to allocate limited hypothetical âdesign resourcesâ among safeguards, recruitment speed, sample size, and system features.
6. One week later, administer a near-transfer vignetteâfor example, using an LLM to code confidential interview transcripts.
7. Conduct purposively sampled interviews from both conditions to examine why particular changes were accepted or rejected.

Do not recruit students through their own instructor if participation could appear connected to grades, supervision, or academic evaluation.

### Primary outcome

The primary outcome should be **actual protocol modification**, not agreement with âethics is important.â

Use blinded expert raters to score revisions from 0â2 on each domain:

- AI-specific informed consent and uncertainty disclosure
- Data minimization, third-party information, retention, and deletion
- Output monitoring and escalation
- Participant withdrawal, recourse, and adverse-event reporting
- Justice, vulnerability, labor, and distribution of burdens
- Model versioning, reproducibility, and accountability

This gives a 0â12 redesign score. Predefine the rubric using Belmontâs respect, beneficence, and justice principles, plus AI-specific accountability ([McKee, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/TTS.2024.3446183)).

Secondary outcomes could include:

- Whether the student would pause recruitment or request further ethics review
- Number and diversity of direct and indirect stakeholders identified
- Uptake of safeguards carrying a visible cost or delay
- Perceived severity and likelihood of harm
- Confidence that modifications will work
- Psychological reactance or perceived moral judgment
- Transfer and retention after one week

Use an ANCOVA or mixed-effects model predicting post-intervention redesign quality from condition while controlling for baseline quality, prior ethics training, and vignette order. Preregister one primary outcome; treat mediation analyses as exploratory unless adequately powered.

### Two-condition limitation

With exactly two active conditions, the experiment can determine which intervention performs better. It cannot cleanly establish that either is better than ordinary ethics instruction or no intervention. A pretest helps, but does not fully replace an active control.

Therefore, phrase the primary claim as:

> âWe compare two mechanisms for supporting ethical redesign.â

If the desired claim is that both interventions independently work, a third neutral-information condition would eventually be necessary.

## Ethical safeguards for this ethics study

The intervention must not reproduce the ethical problems it critiques:

- Use synthetic diary excerpts; never ask researchers to disclose real sensitive information.
- Do not use a live LLM during the main experiment. Pre-render and human-review every AI output so all participants receive controlled content.
- Co-design and validate the role scripts with former research participants, junior research workers, ethics reviewers, and relevant affected communitiesâand compensate them.
- Explicitly state that role-play cannot provide authentic access to another personâs lived experience.
- Offer a content warning, withdrawal without penalty, debriefing, and support information.
- Keep individual answers confidential from supervisors and instructors.
- Seek institutional ethics approval before recruitment. SIGCHI requires human-subjects work to complete the appropriate institutional approval process ([SIGCHI, 2023](https://sigchi.org/about/policies/conference-policies/submission-and-review/)).

## Expected âmore ethicalâ redesign

A strong revision should normally include:

- Explicit consent about the LLM, provider, purpose, uncertainty, and possible model changes
- Local preprocessing and removal of identifiers and third-party information before any model call
- Verified contractual retention and deletion arrangements
- No unreviewed personalized mental-health advice from an unvalidated general-purpose LLM
- Trained human oversight and a proportionate escalation protocol
- Clear stopping rules, incident logging, withdrawal, deletion, and recourse
- Protection and support for research assistants handling distressing material
- Model/version/prompt documentation for reproducibility
- Independent ethics review and ongoing monitoring rather than approval as a one-time event

## CHI 2027 positioning and feasibility

A possible title is:

> **Would You Approve It If You Could Be Anyone? Comparing Near-Future Consequence Forecasting and Role-Reversal Walkthroughs for Ethical AI Research Design**

Potential contributions are:

1. A controlled comparison of two mechanism-distinct ethics interventions.
2. The Role-Reversal Stakeholder Mirror as a reusable HCI method.
3. A behavioral rubric for measuring ethical research redesign rather than ethical attitudes.
4. Evidence about transfer, durability, reactance, and failure modes.

There is a serious timing constraint. As of August 15, 2026, the [CHI 2027 paper deadline is September 10, 2026](https://chi2027.acm.org/authors/papers/). Unless ethics approval, piloting, and recruitment are already underway, a full empirical paper should not be rushed. The [Poster and Student Research Competition deadlines are January 21, 2027](https://chi2027.acm.org/authors/posters/), making a carefully evaluated pilot or design artifact more realistic.
